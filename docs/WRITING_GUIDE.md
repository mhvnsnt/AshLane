# AshLane Writing Guide

**Rule zero:** If it sounds like a chatbot wrote it, rewrite it. When someone reads AshLane
dialogue, they should feel a PERSON behind it — someone who's been on a block, sat on a stoop,
waited on a corner.

The Bannon books were co-written with an AI and it shows: polished sentences, hollow center.
AshLane does the opposite. Rough edges are a feature.

---

## 1. What we steal from the masters

### Yakuza — sincerity over cool

Yakuza's substories are called "the most human narratives in video games" for a reason.
The formula:

- **Play melodrama straight.** Nobody winks. A man crying over a stolen kids' game is treated
  with the same gravity as a clan war. The sincerity IS the joke and the heart at the same time.
- **Empathy first.** Kiryu genuinely tries to understand everyone — the drunk, the scammer,
  the idiot. Ash never looks down on an NPC, no matter how small their problem.
- **Small stakes, big feelings.** A lost wallet. A kid getting bullied. A food cart about to
  close. These hit harder than "save the city" because they're SPECIFIC.
- **Let characters be stupid.** Real people say dumb things, misunderstand each other,
  make bad jokes. Yakuza lets them. AI writing makes everyone sound clever. Don't.

**AshLane take:** Side missions ("?") should feel like Yakuza substories — petty squabbles,
small victories, a laugh and a gut-punch in the same three minutes.

### Def Jam: Fight for NY — profanity with a purpose

GameSpot's review nailed it: the dialogue was profane but "rarely ever seems forced...
the profanity always fits into the context of the situation."

- **Real voices.** Def Jam hired actual rappers. The lesson for us: every character should
  sound like they come from SOMEWHERE. Accent, slang, rhythm — not generic "tough guy."
- **Swearing is punctuation, not decoration.** A character who curses every other word
  sounds like AI doing "street." A character who curses ONCE, at the exact right moment,
  sounds dangerous.
- **Short sentences hit harder.** Def Jam dialogue is clipped. Nobody monologues.
  Threats are two sentences max.

**AshLane take:** Dialogue is short, specific, and regional. Curse like a person, not a
parody.

### Urban Reign — economy

Urban Reign barely talks. Mission briefings are a few lines: who, where, why.
It works because:

- **No wasted words.** Every line moves you toward the fight or tells you something
  about the world.
- **Let the fighting talk.** Character comes through in HOW someone fights, not
  what they say before it.

**AshLane take:** Briefings stay tight. Save the words for moments that earn them.

## 2. Voice and tone rules

1. **Street, not corporate.** Nobody says "utilize." Nobody says "at the end of the day."
   Corporate speak is the enemy.
2. **Specific, not generic.** Not "this neighborhood is dangerous." Instead: "Don't cut
   through the lot behind the laundromat after nine. Ask Dee what happened to his cousin."
3. **Concrete nouns.** Names of streets, foods, stores, people. AI writes "the city."
   Humans write "the corner by the chicken spot on 47th."
4. **Uneven rhythm.** Real speech stutters, repeats, trails off. Not every line is a
   complete sentence. Fragments are fine. So are interruptions.
5. **No purple prose.** "The crimson sun bled over the concrete jungle" is AI slop.
   "Sun's going down. We move in ten." is a person.
6. **Show the scar, don't describe the wound.** Nobody explains their trauma in a speech.
   It leaks out sideways — a flinch, a joke that lands wrong, changing the subject.
7. **Every faction talks different.** (See section 4.)

## 3. Dialogue do's and don'ts

### DON'T — AI-feeling

> ENFORCER: "You have made a grave mistake coming to my territory, Ash Lane.
> I am the undisputed champion of these streets, and I will show you no mercy."

Why it's bad: no human talks like this. "Grave mistake." "Undisputed champion of these
streets." "Show you no mercy." It's a movie trailer, not a person. Every word is generic —
swap in any name and any city and nothing changes.

### DO — human-feeling

> ENFORCER: "Ash. Man. You really walked your ass down here?"
> ASH: "We need to talk about the lot."
> ENFORCER: "...Yeah. Okay. We can talk."

Why it works: It's SHORT. There's history in what's NOT said. "You really walked your
ass down here?" tells you everything about their relationship and the stakes without
explaining anything.

### DON'T — AI-feeling

> NPC: "Thank you so much for your assistance, kind stranger! You have saved my
> business, and I am eternally grateful. Please, take this reward as a token of
> my appreciation."

Why it's bad: Nobody is this formal on the street. "Kind stranger." "Eternally grateful."
"Token of my appreciation." This is a fantasy RPG innkeeper, not a person.

### DO — human-feeling

> NPC: "Yo. Yo, for real? ...Damn. Look, I ain't got much, but — here. Take it.
> And if anybody asks, you never saw my cart."

Why it works: Hesitation. The "yo, for real?" — disbelief. "I ain't got much" — pride
and shame in four words. The last line tells you he's still scared. People don't stop
being scared just because you helped them.

### DON'T — AI-feeling

> VILLAIN: "For years I have waited in the shadows, building my empire piece by
> piece. And now, at last, the final phase of my plan begins!"

Why it's bad: Exposition dump disguised as dialogue. Real villains don't narrate
their five-year plan. (Kennedy especially — see his voice below.)

### DO — human-feeling

> KENNEDY: "You know what your problem is, Ashton? You think this is about you."

Why it works: Dismissive. Personal. He doesn't explain the plan — he doesn't think
you're worth explaining it to. Contempt is scarier than exposition.

## 4. Character voices — how they actually sound

Every major character gets a fingerprint: vocabulary, rhythm, what they NEVER say.

### Ashton "Ash" Lane — quiet, direct, tired

- Short sentences. Doesn't waste words. Listens more than he talks.
- Never gives speeches. When he does say something long, it lands because it's rare.
- Dry humor, almost accidental.
- **Sounds like:** "I'm not here for that." / "You done?" / "...Yeah. I know."
- **Never sounds like:** a motivational poster. Ash doesn't say "we ride together"
  or "the block remembers" out loud — that's the game's theme, not his dialogue.

### Sombra Negra — precise, cold, professional

- Speaks like a contract is being read. Every word weighed.
- Doesn't curse much. Doesn't need to.
- **Sounds like:** "You have ten seconds to leave." / "That was your warning."
- **Never:** raises his voice, jokes, explains himself.

### Captain Silas "The System" (Dynasty Authority) — bureaucratic menace

- Talks like paperwork. "Pursuant to." "In violation of." Weaponized formality.
- The scary part: he's calm. Always calm.
- **Sounds like:** "You're in violation of the Peace Act, section four. Come quietly
  and this stays simple." / "I don't make the rules, son. I just enforce them."
- **Never:** loses his temper. Never swears. That's what makes him terrifying —
  everyone else yells, Silas files.

### "Big Dawg" Titus — big, warm, then not

- Friendly. Laughs easy. Sounds like the nicest guy in the room.
- The turn: when the smile drops, the sentences get shorter.
- **Sounds like:** "Haha! Look at you, all fired up! ...Alright. Alright, let's go."
  / "I asked you nice. That was me asking nice."
- **Never:** cold from the start. The warmth is real — which is why the violence
  is scary.

### Kiko Tanaka — theatrical, playful, unhinged edges

- Great Muta influence: dramatic, performative. Talks like he's on stage.
- Switches tone mid-sentence — giggling to menacing.
- **Sounds like:** "Ooh, a fighter! Come, come — show Uncle Kiko what you got!
  ...Don't disappoint me." / "Shining... WIZARD!"
- **Never:** quiet. Kiko is never quiet.

### Cain Elias — says almost nothing

- Undertaker influence: presence over words.
- When he speaks, it's final. No repeats, no elaboration.
- **Sounds like:** "Leave." / "You shouldn't have come here." / "...Done."
- **Never:** explains, threatens at length, or speaks more than one sentence at a time.
- His police connection NEVER comes up in his dialogue. Not once. Not a hint.

### Edwin J. Kennedy — contempt in a suit

- Doesn't yell. Doesn't threaten directly. Talks DOWN.
- Asks questions he doesn't want answered.
- **Sounds like:** "Do you have any idea what you cost me today?" / "You're
  not a player, Ashton. You're an expense."
- **Never:** monologues about the plan. Never says "you fool." He's worse than that —
  he's disappointed.

### Grunts — give them ONE thing

Every generated grunt gets a single verbal tic or attitude, not a personality essay:

- The nervous one: "Look, man, I don't want no trouble, but—"
- The showoff: "Y'all watching? WATCH this."
- The tired one: "Can we just... get this over with?"
- The loyal one: "You touch him, you go through me."

One line of flavor. That's all a grunt needs. Yakuza proved it.

## 5. Scene-level rules

1. **Enter late, leave early.** Start the scene as close to the conflict as possible.
   End the second the point lands. No hellos, no goodbyes unless they mean something.
2. **One scene, one job.** A scene either advances plot, reveals character, or sets
   mood. Never all three. Never zero.
3. **Conflict in every scene.** Even friendly scenes need friction — a disagreement,
   an awkward silence, something unsaid. Comfort is boring.
4. **Don't narrate what the player can see.** If a character is bleeding, nobody says
   "you're bleeding." They say "sit down before you fall down."
5. **Silence is dialogue.** A beat where nobody talks says more than a paragraph.
   Write [beat] into scripts. Trust it.
6. **Subtext over text.** Nobody says what they mean. "We should talk" means "you're
   in trouble." "Be careful out there" means "I love you, idiot."

## 6. The AI-tell checklist

Run every line of dialogue through this before it ships:

- [ ] Could this line appear in ANY game? If yes, rewrite it.
- [ ] Does anyone speak in complete, polished paragraphs? Break them up.
- [ ] Are there three adjectives where one would do? Cut two.
- [ ] Does the villain explain their plan? Delete the explanation.
- [ ] Does anyone say "my friend," "kind stranger," or "at the end of the day"? Delete.
- [ ] Is the emotion NAMED ("I am so angry") instead of SHOWN? Show it.
- [ ] Would a real person from this neighborhood say this out loud? If not, rewrite.
- [ ] Read it aloud. Does it sound like a person talking or an essay? If essay, rewrite.

## 7. What "human" actually means here

It's not about slang. It's not about cursing. Plenty of AI writing has slang and
cursing and still feels dead.

Human writing has:

- **Hesitation.** People don't know what they're going to say until they say it.
- **Specificity.** The laundromat on 47th, not "the city."
- **Contradiction.** The tough guy who's scared. The cop who cries. The killer
  who tips well.
- **Unsaid things.** The best line in a scene is often the one nobody says.
- **Rhythm.** Short. Short. Long sentence that runs because the character can't
  stop once they start — then. Stop.

Write like someone's listening. Because they are.
